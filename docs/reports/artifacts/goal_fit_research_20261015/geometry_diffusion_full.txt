Abstract
Sparse-view computed tomography (CT) reduces radiation dose and acquisition time and may mitigate motion artifacts. However, angular undersampling provides insufficient information to determine the image uniquely and stably. Limited-angle CT, arising from restricted angular coverage, produces strongly directional information loss associated with the missing angular range. In both settings, image directions may be strongly observed, weakly constrained, or unobservable, leading to severe ill-posedness and reconstruction ambiguity. Existing diffusion-based approaches incorporate measurement information through likelihood guidance, data-consistency operations, or range–null-space corrections. However, they do not generally use the continuously varying measurement sensitivity of the acquisition to jointly shape both reconstruction updates and stochastic exploration.
We propose a geometry-aware diffusion-guided stochastic reconstruction framework for sparse-view and limited-angle CT. Its central component is a regularized noise-weighted pullback metric constructed from the CT forward operator and measurement-noise covariance. This metric continuously adapts both the measurement-aware update and stochastic exploration according to directional measurement sensitivity, suppressing changes along strongly constrained directions while permitting greater exploration along weakly constrained and unobservable directions. We complement this geometry-aware update with a regularized data-consistency correction and approximately null-space-restricted stochastic perturbations, implemented matrix-free using forward and backprojection operations together with conjugate-gradient solves. Experiments on sparse-view, noisy, and limited-angle CT demonstrate competitive reconstruction quality, strong measurement consistency, and spatially resolved empirical uncertainty estimates.
1 Introduction
Reducing radiation dose and acquisition time remains a central objective in CT and motivates sparse-view imaging, in which projections are acquired at only a small number of angles. Limited-angle CT arises from a different acquisition constraint: projections are unavailable over a contiguous angular range because of physical or geometric restrictions. Although the two settings produce different patterns of information loss, both leave many image directions unobservable or weakly constrained. Depending on the acquisition geometry and discretization, the corresponding forward operator may be rank deficient or severely ill-conditioned, resulting in an exact null space or a collection of directions that have only a small effect on the measurements.
From an inverse-problem perspective, this loss of information creates intrinsic ambiguity. Different images may produce identical noiseless measurements or measurement differences too small to distinguish from noise. The measured sinogram therefore cannot uniquely and stably determine the unknown image, and prior information or regularization is required to obtain a plausible reconstruction [52, 3].
Classical CT reconstruction methods include analytical methods such as filtered back projection (FBP) [26] and iterative methods such as ART [16], SART [2], and statistical model-based iterative reconstruction [13, 49]. Under incomplete acquisition, iterative methods commonly combine a data-fidelity term with regularization based on assumptions such as image smoothness, sparsity, or piecewise constancy. Representative examples include total-variation and other sparsity-promoting formulations [41, 12]. Although these methods improve stability, their performance depends on the selected regularizer, and overly restrictive regularization may remove fine anatomical structures, particularly under severe angular undersampling [63, 50]. Moreover, these methods usually produce a single reconstruction and do not quantify the uncertainty in image components that are weakly constrained or unobservable from the measurements [25, 47].
Between classical regularization and fully learned reconstruction, plug-and-play priors [54] and regularization by denoising [37] incorporate an external denoiser into an iterative reconstruction algorithm. These frameworks are not inherently learning-based, because the denoiser may be either handcrafted or learned.
More recently, learning-based methods have introduced data-driven image models into CT reconstruction. Representative approaches include supervised reconstruction networks [24], unrolled reconstruction networks [1], deep image priors [53, 33], and implicit neural representations [42, 44]. The latter two
approaches optimize a network for the observed data and therefore do not necessarily require paired training images.
These methods can achieve strong reconstruction quality
[40]. However, supervised methods require representative training data and may be sensitive to changes in anatomy, acquisition geometry, or noise level. Methods such as deep image priors and implicit neural representations do not require training on an external dataset. Instead, they represent the unknown image with a neural network whose parameters are fitted directly to each input sinogram through the CT forward model. Moreover, many of these approaches return a single reconstruction and do not directly characterize ambiguity caused by missing or noisy measurements.
Diffusion and score-based models learn distributions of clean images and can therefore provide learned prior information for inverse problems. At each diffusion timestep, a pretrained diffusion model estimates the score of the noisy-image distribution obtained from clean CT images. This score provides a time-dependent learned prior direction that guides reconstruction toward plausible clean images. Measurement information can then be incorporated through conditional reverse transitions, likelihood-gradient guidance, or explicit data-consistency operations [46, 27, 7]. For example, DDRM constructs operator-conditioned reverse transitions for linear inverse problems [27]; DPS incorporates the
measurement likelihood through gradient guidance [7]; DDS incorporates conjugate-gradient data-consistency updates into diffusion sampling [8]; and ReSample applies an optimization-based hard data-consistency step followed by stochastic resampling [43].
Diffusion models have also been applied to incomplete-view CT through sinogram restoration, image-domain degradation models, likelihood-guided sampling, and iterative data-consistency corrections [60, 6, 32, 17].
A related family of methods exploits range–null-space structure, separating image components that affect the measured sinogram from components that are invisible to the acquisition. DDNM [57] uses a pseudoinverse to impose data consistency on the measurement-sensitive range-space component of each
diffusion estimate while retaining null-space content predicted by the diffusion model. RN-SDE [18] and its noise-aware extension RNSD+ [19] use a trained diffusion process to recover information missing from limited-angle measurements and repeatedly correct
the component that is visible in the acquired data. The conditional score-based null-space (CSN) model [62] similarly uses a score model to estimate missing image information while using an iterative least-squares step to enforce agreement with the measured projections. Like DDNM, these methods use range–null-space-based corrections that treat the data-visible and measurement-invisible components separately: measurement information corrects the range-space component, whereas the learned diffusion model estimates or retains the complementary null-space component.
In contrast, our method does not make a binary range–null-space separation its primary sampling mechanism. It uses a regularized noise-weighted pullback metric to continuously adapt both guided updates and stochastic exploration across image directions according to their measurement sensitivity. Approximate null-space exploration and regularized data-consistency correction are complementary components of this geometry-aware framework.
Although these methods improve incomplete-view CT reconstruction, they do
not fully account for the fact that incomplete measurements constrain some
image directions strongly, other directions only weakly, and exact
null-space directions not at all. Existing methods use the forward operator
through likelihood guidance, data-consistency optimization, or separate
range–null-space corrections. However, they do not generally use the same continuously varying measurement sensitivity to govern both the measurement-aware update and
stochastic exploration.
In this work, we develop a geometry-aware diffusion-guided stochastic
reconstruction framework for sparse-view and limited-angle CT. Its central
component is a regularized noise-weighted pullback metric constructed from
the CT forward operator and measurement-noise covariance. The metric
continuously adapts the measurement-aware update and stochastic exploration
according to the sensitivity of each image direction to the acquired data:
directions that strongly affect the measured sinogram receive smaller
updates and less stochastic variation, whereas weakly constrained directions
receive relatively greater exploration. The framework additionally includes
approximate null-space exploration and regularized data-consistency
correction, both evaluated matrix-free through forward and backprojection
operations together with conjugate-gradient solves.

2 Related Work
Diffusion priors for inverse problems.
Diffusion and score-based generative models provide learned image priors for ill-posed inverse problems. The score-based SDE framework formulates generation as a reverse-time stochastic process and provides a general basis for incorporating measurement information during sampling [46]. DDRM constructs operator-conditioned reverse transitions for linear inverse problems [27], whereas DPS
uses approximate likelihood-gradient guidance for noisy linear and nonlinear inverse problems [7]. MCG and Π\PiGDM refine conditional-score guidance through manifold-constrained and pseudoinverse-inspired constructions, respectively [9, 45]. Other approaches combine pretrained diffusion models with optimization or consistency operations, including DDS,
ReSample, PSLD, and DMPlug [8, 43, 38, 56].
Riemannian MCMC methods.
Riemannian Langevin methods form a class of Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods in which a metric determines how far the sampler moves and how much random perturbation it adds in different parameter-space directions [15]. Thus, they provide a general principle for using geometry to shape both deterministic and stochastic sampling components. Our framework uses this principle in a different setting: diffusion-guided reconstruction from incomplete CT measurements. Rather than constructing a general state-dependent posterior metric, we derive a noise-weighted pullback geometry from the CT forward operator and measurement-noise covariance. This geometry represents the directional sensitivity of the particular CT acquisition and is used to adapt the measurement-aware update and stochastic exploration during reconstruction.
Diffusion models for incomplete-view CT.
Several diffusion models have been developed specifically for sparse-view and limited-angle CT. CT-SDM models view reduction through a deterministic sinogram-domain degradation process, whereas CvG-Diff learns to reverse image-domain artifacts caused by angular undersampling
[60, 6]. Other methods use sinogram-domain generation, spatial-frequency representations, projection conditioning, or physics-informed score models
[32, 17, 35, 20]. These approaches show that diffusion priors can effectively reduce incomplete-view artifacts. They typically encode the acquisition geometry through a learned degradation, restoration, or conditioning process. In
contrast, our framework incorporates the CT forward operator and measurement-noise model directly into the reconstruction dynamics. The resulting noise-weighted pullback geometry continuously adapts both the measurement-aware update and stochastic exploration according to directional measurement sensitivity.
Data consistency and range–null-space methods.
Explicit data-consistency operations are widely used in diffusion-guided reconstruction. DDS combines diffusion sampling with conjugate-gradient data-consistency updates, while ReSample alternates diffusion generation, optimization-based consistency correction, and resampling
[8, 43]. These operations can reduce disagreement with the acquired data. However, by themselves, they do not provide a common continuously varying noise-weighted
geometry for both the measurement-aware reconstruction update and stochastic exploration across strongly and weakly observed image directions.
A related family of methods uses range–null-space structure. For each diffusion estimate, DDNM uses the acquired measurements and a pseudoinverse to correct the image content that affects the measurements. It retains the remaining measurement-invisible content from the diffusion model [57]. RN-SDE uses a conditional mean-reverting diffusion process to reconstruct limited-angle CT images and repeatedly applies a range–null-space correction: the data-visible content is adjusted using the acquired measurements, while the missing content is inferred by the learned model [18]. Its extension RNSD+ addresses noisy limited-angle CT by treating missing views as a sinogram-inpainting problem and introducing a noise-aware
rectification mechanism [19]. Similarly, CSN combines conditional score-based reconstruction with iterative least-squares data consistency so that missing information is estimated principally in the null space [62]. These methods conceptually treat data-visible and measurement-invisible components separately.
In contrast, our framework does not first decompose each diffusion estimate into range-space and null-space components in order to construct its primary sampling update. Instead, the main diffusion-guided update is defined over the whole image space and is continuously adapted by a regularized noise-weighted pullback metric. We then use complementary range–null-space-inspired operations: a regularized data-consistency
correction modifies the measurement-sensitive component, whereas an approximately null-space-restricted perturbation promotes additional exploration in weakly constrained and unobservable directions. Thus, range–null-space structure is used in supporting data-consistency and null-space-exploration steps rather than as a binary decomposition underlying the main update.
3 Method
In sparse-view and limited-angle CT, the measurements constrain some image directions more, while other directions are less constrained or unobservable. We therefore propose a diffusion-score-guided stochastic reconstruction framework that uses the CT forward operator and noise covariance to adapt both the direction and scale of each sampling update according to noise-weighted measurement sensitivity. The framework reduces the measurement residual through a regularized data-consistency correction. At the same time it concentrates stochastic exploration along weakly constrained and unobservable directions, where reconstruction ambiguity remains.
The proposed method is presented in six parts. We first introduce the forward model, the noise-weighted posterior, and the resulting anisotropy between strongly observed, weakly constrained, and unobservable image directions in Section 3.1. Section 3.2 uses this geometry to construct pullback-metric-preconditioned Langevin dynamics in place of standard isotropic sampling. Section 3.3 describes the orthogonal decomposition of the image space into range-space (containing directions that affect the measurements) and null-space (containing directions that are invisible to the forward operator). Section 3.4 introduces a regularized Riemannian data-consistency correction that avoids requiring an explicitly available generalized inverse. Section 3.5 introduces approximately null-space-restricted stochastic perturbations that promote exploration along weakly constrained and unobservable image directions while suppressing changes in the measured sinogram.
Finally, Section 3.6 combines all components into an iterative sampling procedure that (i) preconditions diffusion updates using the measurement-induced pullback metric, (ii) reduces the measurement residual through a regularized data-consistency correction, and (iii) controls stochastic exploration according to the measurement sensitivity of different image directions, thus supporting spatial uncertainty estimation.
3.1 Problem Formulation and Posterior Geometry
In the context of CT reconstruction, our goal is to recover images from projection measurements acquired through a forward operator. The CT measurements constrain different image directions to different degrees: some directions strongly affect the measured sinogram, whereas others are weakly constrained or completely unobservable. We formulate sparse-view and limited-angle CT reconstruction as Bayesian inference. A posterior formulation combines the information provided by the measurements with a learned image prior and allows the remaining ambiguity to be represented by multiple reconstruction samples.

3.2 Geometry-Aware Diffusion Sampling via a Pullback Metric
As discussed in Section 3.1, the likelihood constrains different image directions to different degrees. It provides strong information along well-observed directions, limited information along weakly constrained directions, and no direct information along exact null-space directions. Standard isotropic Langevin dynamics [29], which combine gradient-based drift with Gaussian noise for posterior sampling [36], do not account for these differences because their stochastic term has the same variance in every image direction. We therefore construct a Riemannian metric from the CT forward operator and effective noise precision to adapt both the deterministic and stochastic updates to measurement sensitivity.
To construct the metric, consider a small image perturbation
δ​x∈ℝn\delta x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}. Under the linear forward model, it produces the measurement perturbation
δ​y=AΩ​δ​x.\delta y\;=\;A_{\Omega}\delta x.

reconstruction, estimated as described in Appendix A.1. Its inverse Σ−1≻0\Sigma^{-1}\succ 0 is called the precision matrix. (12)

⟨u,v⟩𝒴=∑i=1m(Σ−1)i​i​ui​vi.\langle u,v\rangle_{\mathcal{Y}}\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\Sigma^{-1})_{ii}u_{i}v_{i}.

Measurements with higher precision receive greater weight, whereas noisier measurements with lower precision receive less weight. Thus, ∥⋅∥𝒴\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{Y}} measures a sinogram perturbation relative to the reliability of the measurements.
Pullback metric on image space.
The inner product ⟨⋅,⋅⟩𝒴\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{Y}} defines a geometry on the measurement space ℝm\mathbb{R}^{m}, but the unknown image xx lives in the image space ℝn\mathbb{R}^{n}. To transfer this geometry to the image space, we use the concept of a pullback metric [30, 11], a standard construction in differential geometry that transports a metric from one space to another via a smooth map. Given a small image
perturbation δ​x∈ℝn\delta x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}, the forward operator AΩA_{\Omega} maps it to the corresponding measurement perturbation δ​y=AΩ​δ​x∈ℝm\delta y=A_{\Omega}\,\delta x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}. G​v= 0for every ​v∈𝒩⁡(AΩ).Gv\;=\;0\qquad\text{for every }v\in\mathcal{N}(A_{\Omega}).
We therefore define the regularized metric

Directions with data-dependent eigenvalues much larger than γ\gamma are only slightly affected and retain their measurement-dependent weighting. Along exact null-space directions, the measurement-induced term vanishes because the measurements contribute no information. Only the isotropic Euclidean regularization γ​I\gamma I remains. By contrast, weakly constrained
directions retain a small measurement-dependent contribution together with this regularization.
Having defined the measurement-induced metric GγG_{\gamma}, we now
incorporate it into posterior sampling. Langevin dynamics provide a natural framework because they combine gradient-based guidance toward a target density with stochastic exploration. We first consider an ideal continuous-time process with access to the exact posterior score and then derive the practical diffusion-guided approximation used in our algorithm.
Pullback-Preconditioned Langevin Dynamics.
Let Xτ∈ℝnX_{\tau}\in\mathbb{R}^{n} denote the random image state at continuous
sampling time τ\tau, and let π⁡(x)=p⁡(x∣y)\pi(x)=p(x\mid y) be the target posterior
density. Standard isotropic overdamped Langevin dynamics combine
deterministic drift along the gradient of the log-target density with
isotropic Brownian noise [36]:
d​Xτ=∇log⁡π​(Xτ)​d​τ+2​d​Wτ,dX_{\tau}\;=\;\nabla\log\pi(X_{\tau})\,d\tau+\sqrt{2}\,dW_{\tau},
(16)
where WτW_{\tau} is an nn-dimensional standard Brownian motion. The first term is the deterministic drift, while the second is the stochastic term. Over a small time interval Δ​τ\Delta\tau, the stochastic term contributes a Gaussian increment with covariance 2​Δ​τ​In2\Delta\tau I_{n}, where In∈ℝn×nI_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n} is the identity matrix on the image space. It therefore applies the same variance in every image direction.
The process is constructed to have π\pi as an invariant density: if
X0X_{0} has density π\pi, then XτX_{\tau} has the same density for every
τ≥0\tau\geq 0, as verified below.
To incorporate the measurement geometry, we precondition both the drift and
the stochastic term using Gγ−1G_{\gamma}^{-1}:
dXτ=Gγ−1∇logπ(Xτ)dτ+2​Gγ−1dWτ.dX_{\tau}\;=\;G_{\gamma}^{-1}\nabla\log\pi(X_{\tau})\,d\tau+\sqrt{2G_{\gamma}^{-1}}\,dW_{\tau}.
(17)
The first term is the
geometry-preconditioned drift, while the second is the geometry-shaped
stochastic term. These dynamics suppress variation along directions with
high noise-weighted measurement sensitivity and permit larger updates along
weakly constrained and unobservable directions.
To ensure that the geometry-aware preconditioning changes the sampling
dynamics without changing the target posterior, we verify that π\pi
remains an invariant density of the ideal continuous process.
Let ρτ​(x)\rho_{\tau}(x) denote the probability density of XτX_{\tau}. For a
stochastic differential equation of the form
d​Xτ=b⁡(Xτ)​d​τ+B​d​Wτ,dX_{\tau}\;=\;b(X_{\tau})\,d\tau+B\,dW_{\tau},
where b:ℝn→ℝnb:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n} is the drift function and
B∈ℝn×nB\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n} is a constant diffusion matrix, the
corresponding Fokker–Planck equation is [34]
∂τρτ=−∇⋅(bρτ)+12∇⋅(BB⊤∇ρτ).\partial_{\tau}\rho_{\tau}\;=\;-\nabla\!\cdot\left(b\,\rho_{\tau}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\nabla\!\cdot\left(BB^{\top}\nabla\rho_{\tau}\right).
For Equation (17),
b(x)=Gγ−1∇logπ(x),BB⊤= 2Gγ−1.b(x)\;=\;G_{\gamma}^{-1}\nabla\log\pi(x),\qquad BB^{\top}\;=\;2G_{\gamma}^{-1}.
Substituting these expressions gives
∂τρτ\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\rho_{\tau}
=−∇⋅(Gγ−1ρτ∇logπ)+∇⋅(Gγ−1∇ρτ).\displaystyle\;=\;-\nabla\!\cdot\left(G_{\gamma}^{-1}\rho_{\tau}\nabla\log\pi\right)+\nabla\!\cdot\left(G_{\gamma}^{-1}\nabla\rho_{\tau}\right).
To test stationarity, set ρτ=π\rho_{\tau}=\pi. Since
∇logπ=∇ππ,andπ∇logπ=∇π,\nabla\log\pi\;=\;\frac{\nabla\pi}{\pi},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\pi\nabla\log\pi\;=\;\nabla\pi,
we obtain
∂τρτ|ρτ=π=−∇⋅(Gγ−1∇π)+∇⋅(Gγ−1∇π)= 0.\displaystyle\left.\partial_{\tau}\rho_{\tau}\right|_{\rho_{\tau}=\pi}\;=\;-\nabla\!\cdot\left(G_{\gamma}^{-1}\nabla\pi\right)+\nabla\!\cdot\left(G_{\gamma}^{-1}\nabla\pi\right)\;=\;0.
Therefore, if X0X_{0} has density π\pi, then XτX_{\tau} has density π\pi
for every τ≥0\tau\geq 0. Hence, π\pi is an invariant density of the ideal
continuous dynamics. A formal statement and the corresponding conditions are provided in Appendix A.5.
Diffusion-score approximation.
The ideal dynamics in Equation (17) require the exact posterior score. From the posterior factorization, this score has the
form
∇log⁡π​(x)=∇log⁡p0​(x)−∇Φ​(x),\nabla\log\pi(x)\;=\;\nabla\log p_{0}(x)-\nabla\Phi(x),
where the data-fidelity gradient ∇Φ​(x)\nabla\Phi(x) can be evaluated from the known forward operator, measured sinogram, and fixed noise precision, whereas the clean-image prior score ∇log⁡p0​(x)\nabla\log p_{0}(x) is not available analytically. We use a denoising diffusion probabilistic model (DDPM) [23] pretrained on clean CT images. During pretraining, Gaussian noise is added to the images at different diffusion timesteps, and the model learns to predict the added noise. During reconstruction, the model parameters remain fixed, and the predicted noise is used to estimate the image-prior score that guides the reconstruction toward plausible CT
images.
Let x(k)x^{(k)} denote the current reconstruction at sampling iteration kk, and let tkt_{k} be the corresponding DDPM timestep. For simplicity, all image variables below are expressed in the normalized intensity range used during diffusion-model training.
At each iteration, we treat x(k)x^{(k)} as the current estimate of the clean image and apply the standard DDPM forward-noising equation:
xtk(k)=α¯tk​x(k)+1−α¯tk​ϵ(k),ϵ(k)∼𝒩⁡(0,In),x_{t_{k}}^{(k)}\;=\;\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t_{k}}}\,x^{(k)}+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t_{k}}}\,\epsilon^{(k)},\qquad\epsilon^{(k)}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{n}),
(18)
where α¯tk\bar{\alpha}_{t_{k}} is the cumulative product of the DDPM noise schedule up to timestep tkt_{k}. The variable xtk(k)x_{t_{k}}^{(k)} is the noisy image supplied to the pretrained diffusion model.
For an ϵ\epsilon-prediction DDPM, the network ϵθ​(xtk(k),tk)\epsilon_{\theta}(x_{t_{k}}^{(k)},t_{k}) predicts the Gaussian noise contained in xtk(k)x_{t_{k}}^{(k)}. The corresponding score estimate is [23, 46]
s(k)=−ϵθ​(xtk(k),tk)1−α¯tk,s^{(k)}\;=\;-\frac{\epsilon_{\theta}(x_{t_{k}}^{(k)},t_{k})}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t_{k}}}},
(19)
which approximates
∇xtk​log​ptk​(xtk(k))\nabla_{x_{t_{k}}}\log p_{t_{k}}(x_{t_{k}}^{(k)}). This score supplies the learned image-prior direction at noise level tkt_{k}.
The data-fidelity gradient is evaluated at the current reconstruction
x(k)x^{(k)}:
g(k):=∇Φ​(x(k))=AΩ⊤​Σ−1​(AΩ​x(k)−y),g^{(k)}\;:=\;\nabla\Phi(x^{(k)})\;=\;A_{\Omega}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}\left(A_{\Omega}x^{(k)}-y\right),
(20)
where
Φ⁡(x)=12​(AΩ​x−y)⊤​Σ−1​(AΩ​x−y).\Phi(x)\;=\;\frac{1}{2}(A_{\Omega}x-y)^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(A_{\Omega}x-y).
(21)
We combine the DDPM score and data-fidelity gradient as
r(k)=λk​s(k)−μk​g(k),r^{(k)}\;=\;\lambda_{k}s^{(k)}-\mu_{k}g^{(k)},
(22)
where λk\lambda_{k} and μk\mu_{k} control the relative strengths of the learned prior and measurement guidance. The DDPM score is evaluated at the noisy image xtk(k)x_{t_{k}}^{(k)}, whereas the data-fidelity gradient is evaluated at the current reconstruction x(k)x^{(k)}. Therefore, r(k)r^{(k)} is used as a practical combined guidance direction rather than an exact diffusion-time posterior score.
Geometry-preconditioned update.
The pullback-metric-preconditioned direction u(k)u^{(k)} is obtained by solving
Gγ​u(k)=r(k),Gγ=AΩ⊤​Σ−1​AΩ+γ​In.G_{\gamma}u^{(k)}\;=\;r^{(k)},\qquad G_{\gamma}\;=\;A_{\Omega}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}A_{\Omega}+\gamma I_{n}.
(23)
The current reconstruction is then updated according to
x~(k)=x(k)+αk​u(k),x~(k)←x~(k)+νG(k),νG(k)∼𝒩⁡(0,2​αk​Gγ−1),\tilde{x}^{(k)}\;=\;x^{(k)}+\alpha_{k}u^{(k)},\qquad\tilde{x}^{(k)}\leftarrow\tilde{x}^{(k)}+\nu_{G}^{(k)},\qquad\nu_{G}^{(k)}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(0,2\alpha_{k}G_{\gamma}^{-1}\right),
(24)
where αk>0\alpha_{k}>0 is the sampling step size. The deterministic term αk​u(k)\alpha_{k}u^{(k)} combines the learned DDPM prior with the
noise-weighted data-fidelity gradient, while νG(k)\nu_{G}^{(k)} provides metric-shaped stochastic exploration.
Because the evolving reconstruction x(k)x^{(k)} is not itself maintained as a DDPM noisy state, we apply the forward-noising equation before each score evaluation. This produces an input whose noise level is consistent with the assigned DDPM timestep tkt_{k}. The resulting score provides annealed prior guidance, ranging from coarse structure at high noise levels to finer
details at low noise levels. This procedure differs from a standard DDPM reverse trajectory, since a newly noised state is constructed from the current reconstruction at each iteration.
Matrix-free implementation via conjugate gradient.

where r∈ℝnr\in\mathbb{R}^{n} is known and

In practice, it is terminated when the residual falls below a prescribed tolerance. The regularization parameter γ\gamma bounds the smallest eigenvalue away from zero and improves numerical conditioning.
To sample

Solving

With a finite CG tolerance, the generated covariance approximates 2​αk​Gγ−12\alpha_{k}G_{\gamma}^{-1}, with accuracy determined by the linear-solve residual. The full correctness proof is given in Appendix A.5, Lemma 2.
The update (24) is anisotropic because the
measurement-induced metric preconditions both the deterministic direction and the stochastic covariance. It suppresses stochastic variation and prior-driven updates along strongly observed directions while allowing greater variation along weakly constrained and unobservable directions. The resulting variability is determined jointly by the measurement
geometry and the learned prior. The regularized data-consistency correction and additional approximately null-space-restricted perturbations are introduced in Sections 3.4 and
3.5, respectively.
3.3 Orthogonal Direct Sum Decomposition
To exploit the geometric structure of AΩA_{\Omega} explicitly, we need a precise definition of how image space splits into measurement-constrained and measurement-invisible directions. The orthogonal direct sum decomposition[39, 4, 5] provides this definition, and the two subspaces identified by it will correspond directly to the two components of the sampling process in Sections 3.4
and 3.5.
The forward operator maps the image space to the measurement space:
AΩ:ℝn→ℝm.A_{\Omega}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}.
(28)
Accordingly, ℛ⁡(AΩ)⊆ℝm\mathcal{R}(A_{\Omega})\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m} lies in the measurement space and describes sinograms that can be produced by the forward model. To describe measurement-sensitive directions in the image
space, we instead use ℛ⁡(AΩ⊤)⊆ℝn\mathcal{R}(A_{\Omega}^{\top})\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}.
Proposition 1 (Orthogonal decomposition).
For any AΩ∈ℝm×nA_{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}, the image space admits the orthogonal direct-sum decomposition
ℝn=ℛ⁡(AΩ⊤)⊕𝒩⁡(AΩ).\mathbb{R}^{n}\;=\;\mathcal{R}(A_{\Omega}^{\top})\oplus\mathcal{N}(A_{\Omega}).
(29)
Equivalently,
ℛ⁡(AΩ⊤)=𝒩​(AΩ)⟂.\mathcal{R}(A_{\Omega}^{\top})\;=\;\mathcal{N}(A_{\Omega})^{\perp}.
(30)
Here, ℛ⁡(AΩ⊤)\mathcal{R}(A_{\Omega}^{\top}) contains image directions that can affect the measurements, whereas 𝒩⁡(AΩ)\mathcal{N}(A_{\Omega}) contains directions that are invisible to the forward operator. A proof is provided in Appendix A.2.
Consequently, every image x∈ℝnx\in\mathbb{R}^{n} has a unique decomposition
x=xR+xN,xR∈ℛ⁡(AΩ⊤),xN∈𝒩⁡(AΩ).x\;=\;x_{R}+x_{N},\qquad x_{R}\in\mathcal{R}(A_{\Omega}^{\top}),\qquad x_{N}\in\mathcal{N}(A_{\Omega}).
(31)
Because AΩ​xN=0A_{\Omega}x_{N}=0, the predicted sinogram depends only on the range-space component:
AΩ​x=AΩ​xR.A_{\Omega}x\;=\;A_{\Omega}x_{R}.
(32)
If the equation AΩ​x=yA_{\Omega}x=y is feasible, then every exactly measurement-consistent image can be written as
x=xdc+v,xdc∈ℛ⁡(AΩ⊤),v∈𝒩⁡(AΩ),x\;=\;x_{\mathrm{dc}}+v,\qquad x_{\mathrm{dc}}\in\mathcal{R}(A_{\Omega}^{\top}),\qquad v\in\mathcal{N}(A_{\Omega}),
(33)
where xdcx_{\mathrm{dc}} is the unique range-space component satisfying AΩ​xdc=yA_{\Omega}x_{\mathrm{dc}}=y. The measurements therefore determine the range-space component under the exact feasible constraint, while the null-space component remains unidentifiable from the measurements alone.
Under the noisy likelihood used in this work, the distinction is not strictly binary. The likelihood provides no information along exact null-space directions, but it may provide only weak information along directions associated with small noise-weighted singular values. Moreover, the learned prior can influence both range-space and null-space components. The decomposition in (31) therefore
describes exact algebraic observability, while the pullback metric also accounts for continuously varying measurement sensitivity.
This structure motivates the two subsequent components of the method. The regularized data-consistency correction in
Section 3.4 acts along
ℛ⁡(AΩ⊤)\mathcal{R}(A_{\Omega}^{\top}), while the stochastic perturbation in Section 3.5 concentrates variation along weakly constrained and unobservable directions.
3.4 Data Consistency via Riemannian Gradient Projection
Although the geometry-aware update incorporates the measurements through the data-fidelity gradient, one Langevin update can move it away from measurement consistency. We therefore seek a correction x+x^{+} that remains close to the intermediate estimate x~\tilde{x} while reducing its mismatch with the measured sinogram yy.
Define the data-fidelity functional and its Euclidean gradient:
Φ⁡(x)=12​(AΩ​x−y)⊤​Σ−1​(AΩ​x−y),∇Φ​(x)=AΩ⊤​Σ−1​(AΩ​x−y).\Phi(x)\;=\;\tfrac{1}{2}(A_{\Omega}x-y)^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(A_{\Omega}x-y),\qquad\nabla\Phi(x)\;=\;A_{\Omega}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(A_{\Omega}x-y).
(34)
Under the pullback metric Gγproj=AΩ⊤​Σ−1​AΩ+γproj​IG_{\gamma_{\mathrm{proj}}}=A_{\Omega}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}A_{\Omega}+\gamma_{\mathrm{proj}}I,
the Riemannian gradient of Φ\Phi is
gradGγprojΦ(x)=Gγproj−1∇Φ(x)=Gγproj−1AΩ⊤Σ−1(AΩx−y).\operatorname{grad}_{G_{\gamma_{\mathrm{proj}}}}\Phi(x)\;=\;{G^{-1}_{\gamma_{\mathrm{proj}}}}\nabla\Phi(x)\;=\;{G^{-1}_{\gamma_{\mathrm{proj}}}}A_{\Omega}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(A_{\Omega}x-y).
(35)
We use a separate regularization parameter γproj>0\gamma_{\mathrm{proj}}>0 for the data-consistency correction.
This parameter controls the trade-off between measurement agreement and numerical stability. It is distinct from the pullback-metric parameter γ\gamma used in the diffusion update and the null-space parameter γnull\gamma_{\mathrm{null}} used in Section 3.5.
For sparse-view and limited-angle CT, AΩA_{\Omega} may be rank deficient or
severely ill-conditioned. We therefore formulate data consistency directly as the regularized proximity problem
x+=arg⁡minx​{12​‖x−x~‖22+τ2​‖AΩ​x−y‖Σ2},‖v‖Σ2:=v⊤​Σ−1​v,x^{+}\;=\;\arg\min_{x}\left\{\tfrac{1}{2}\|x-\tilde{x}\|_{2}^{2}+\tfrac{\tau}{2}\|A_{\Omega}x-y\|_{\Sigma}^{2}\right\},\qquad\|v\|_{\Sigma}^{2}:=v^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}v,
(36)
where τ>0\tau>0 controls the strength of data consistency.
To obtain the minimizer, we define
J⁡(x)=12​‖x−x~‖22+τ2​(AΩ​x−y)⊤​Σ−1​(AΩ​x−y).J(x)\;=\;\tfrac{1}{2}\|x-\tilde{x}\|_{2}^{2}+\tfrac{\tau}{2}(A_{\Omega}x-y)^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(A_{\Omega}x-y).

Equation (41) is also one unit

(42)
so the data-consistency correction and the Langevin drift use the same pullback geometry. Since Gγproj{G_{\gamma_{\mathrm{proj}}}} is symmetric positive definite for every γproj>0\gamma_{\mathrm{proj}}>0, the correction is well-defined regardless of the rank of AΩA_{\Omega}. For finite γproj\gamma_{\mathrm{proj}}, it improves measurement consistency but does not generally enforce AΩ​x+=yA_{\Omega}x^{+}=y exactly.

This is complementary to the geometry-aware diffusion update, in which the projection enforces near-hard measurement fidelity, while the diffusion dynamics govern prior-driven reconstruction and uncertainty exploration in the null-space.
Remark (Regularized data consistency under noisy measurements).
For a measured sinogram yy, define the exact consistency set
𝒞y:={x∈ℝn:AΩ​x=y}.\mathcal{C}_{y}\;:=\;\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:A_{\Omega}x=y\right\}.
(43)
This set is nonempty if and only if
y∈ℛ⁡(AΩ)y\in\mathcal{R}(A_{\Omega}). When it is nonempty,
𝒞y\mathcal{C}_{y} is a closed affine subspace of ℝn\mathbb{R}^{n},
rather than a curved manifold. If y∉ℛ⁡(AΩ)y\notin\mathcal{R}(A_{\Omega}),
then no image can reproduce the measured sinogram exactly.
Under the noisy measurement model y=AΩ​x⋆+εy=A_{\Omega}x^{\star}+\varepsilon, exact consistency may be infeasible
because the noise realization ε\varepsilon need not lie in
ℛ⁡(AΩ)\mathcal{R}(A_{\Omega}). Even when the constraint is feasible, enforcing AΩ​x+=yA_{\Omega}x^{+}=y exactly fits the noisy measurement and may introduce noise-related features into the reconstruction.
The update in (41) is therefore implemented as a regularized data-consistency correction rather than an exact projection. It is well-defined for every
γproj>0\gamma_{\mathrm{proj}}>0, regardless of the rank of AΩA_{\Omega} or the feasibility of the exact constraint. Smaller values of
γproj\gamma_{\mathrm{proj}} produce stronger agreement with the measurements along observable directions, whereas larger values give a weaker and more strongly regularized correction with improved numerical conditioning.
3.5 Approximately Null-Space-Restricted Stochastic Sampling
The regularized data-consistency correction in Section 3.4 acts along measurement-sensitive directions in ℛ⁡(AΩ⊤)\mathcal{R}(A_{\Omega}^{\top}) and does not directly modify the exact null-space component. Reconstruction ambiguity may therefore remain along exact null-space directions and along directions that are only weakly constrained relative to the measurement noise. To explore this ambiguity, we introduce stochastic perturbations that retain more variation along weakly constrained and unobservable directions while suppressing measurement-sensitive components.
Exact null-space invariance.
Suppose that xx satisfies AΩ​x=yA_{\Omega}x=y. For any
v∈𝒩⁡(AΩ)v\in\mathcal{N}(A_{\Omega}),
AΩ​(x+v)=AΩ​x+AΩ​v=y.A_{\Omega}(x+v)\;=\;A_{\Omega}x+A_{\Omega}v\;=\;y.
(44)
Thus, an exact null-space perturbation does not change the predicted sinogram. An exact orthogonal projection onto
𝒩⁡(AΩ)\mathcal{N}(A_{\Omega}) can be expressed using the Moore–Penrose
pseudoinverse of the discretized forward operator. Although this
pseudoinverse exists mathematically, accurately applying it can be
computationally demanding for large-scale and ill-conditioned CT systems. We therefore use a regularized approximation based on forward and backprojection operations.

At reconstruction iteration kk, we draw

(45)

(46)
Requiring exact null-space membership would give

(48)
Because AΩ​AΩ⊤A_{\Omega}A_{\Omega}^{\top} may be singular or severely

(AΩ​AΩ⊤+γnull​Σ)​q(k)=AΩ​ζ(k),γnull>0.\left(A_{\Omega}A_{\Omega}^{\top}+\gamma_{\mathrm{null}}\Sigma\right)q^{(k)}\;=\;A_{\Omega}\zeta^{(k)},\qquad\gamma_{\mathrm{null}}>0.

4 Experiments
We evaluate the proposed geometry-aware approximate diffusion posterior sampling framework on sparse-view and limited-angle CT reconstruction. The experiments have three goals. First, we assess reconstruction quality by comparing against the DM4CT benchmark [40] using the standard distortion
metrics PSNR and SSIM, and additionally provide qualitative
comparisons across a range of acquisition degradations. Second, we evaluate whether the proposed method produces informative empirical sample variability rather than only a single point estimate, analysing the uncertainty structure of our method. Third, we verify the central geometry-aware claim: that posterior variability is suppressed in measurement space and concentrated in directions that are either invisible to the acquisition geometry or corrupted by measurement noise.
Experiments are conducted on the medical CT data from the 2016 Low Dose Grand Challenge, following the data preprocessing and evaluation protocol used in DM4CT. Unless otherwise stated, stochastic reconstructions are generated from the same measured sinogram. For noisy configurations, the measurement noise realization is fixed and only the sampler seed varies between samples, ensuring that observed variability reflects posterior uncertainty rather than changes in the measurement itself.
4.1 Reconstruction Performance
We evaluate reconstruction performance from two complementary
perspectives. First, we compare against existing methods on the standard DM4CT benchmark, reporting PSNR and SSIM to enable direct quantitative comparison with the published benchmark results (Section 4.1.1).
Second, we provide qualitative comparisons across a controlled set of acquisition degradations, to isolate how different kinds of ill-posedness affect reconstruction quality (Section 4.1.2).
Together, these two evaluations establish that the proposed method
achieves competitive reconstruction quality across the range of CT acquisition conditions in DM4CT.
4.1.1 Quantitative Benchmark Comparison
We follow the standard DM4CT benchmark configurations to enable direct comparison with existing CT reconstruction methods. The five configurations are: (i) 40 projections without noise, (ii) 20 projection angles with mild noise, (iii) 80 projections with higher noise, (iv) 80 projections with mild noise and ring artifacts, and (v) 40 projections with limited-angle views [0,34​π][0,\frac{3}{4}\pi]. All methods are evaluated under the same forward operator, preprocessing, and metric computation protocol as DM4CT for fair comparison.

Supervised methods: a state-of-the-art supervised reconstruction network trained on paired data [31].
Diffusion-based methods: representative posterior sampling and data-consistency strategies, including DDS [8], DPS [7], PGDM [45], and ReSample [43].

This increase in margin from Config i to Config v is consistent with the role of geometry-aware guidance. In the sparse-view setting, the null-space is induced by missing views but is less directionally structured, so the advantage of geometry-aware comes primarily from the pullback metric redirecting updates away from measurement-sensitive directions. In the limited-angle setting, the null-space is strongly anisotropic and aligned with the missing angular range. The pullback metric explicitly accounts for this geometry, providing a stronger advantage as the null-space becomes more geometrically structured. This is also reflected in the behavior of ReSample, which drops from 32.03 dB in
Config i to 27.70 dB in Config v, indicating that hard projection
alone is insufficient for structured null-spaces.
For the noisy sparse-view settings, the proposed method remains competitive and is particularly strong among posterior-sampling methods. In Config ii, it achieves 28.98 dB/0.81 SSIM, outperforming DPS, DDS, PGDM, and ReSample. In Config iii, the acquisition contains more projection views but a higher noise level. The increased angular coverage reduces the geometric
ambiguity and therefore limits the relative advantage of null-space-aware sampling. Reconstruction quality is instead determined mainly by the balance between noise suppression and measurement consistency. In addition, the effective measurement precision is based on a post-log Gaussian approximation that is most reliable at moderate-to-high photon counts. Under the stronger noise in Config iii, deviations from this approximation may lead to less accurate likelihood weighting and reduce
reconstruction performance. Consequently, the proposed method obtains a slightly lower PSNR than MCG (28.83 versus 28.90 dB). However, it achieves substantially higher SSIM (0.80 versus 0.71), indicating better preservation of structural information.
Under ring artifacts (Config iv), the proposed method obtains
29.42 dB/0.81, giving one of the strongest structural reconstructions among diffusion-based methods. Resample is slightly higher in PSNR, while the proposed method maintains a higher SSIM. One possible explanation is that the projection-consistency term forces the reconstruction to match the measured sinogram. When the sinogram contains ring artifacts, this can also force the reconstruction to keep some of these artifacts, which may lower the PSNR. This suggests that ring artifacts may require artifact-aware noise modelling or adaptive precision weighting, so that unreliable sinogram components are not treated with the same confidence as clean measurements.
Since posterior-sampling methods are stochastic, their performance can depend on the particular sample drawn from the posterior. To assess this variability, we additionally generate 20 stochastic reconstruction samples for DDS, DPS, PGDM, ReSample, and the proposed method on one representative test slice for each acquisition configuration. We select DDS [8], DPS [7], PGDM [45], and ReSample [43] for this analysis because they achieve competitive performance in the benchmark and can generate multiple stochastic reconstruction samples. They therefore provide appropriate baselines for assessing both reconstruction accuracy and sampling variability. The posterior mean and sample variability are reported in Appendix B.1. This analysis is not
intended to replace the benchmark-style comparison in Table 2, but to provide additional insight into the stability of the posterior samplers.

FBP is included as an analytical reference to the reconstruction difficulty of each setting rather than as a competitive baseline.

(iv) 40 projections with limited angular range [0,π/2][0,\pi/2], and
(v) 10 projections with mild Poisson noise.

acquisition settings. DDS performs well when the measurements are noise-free: in setting (i), it recovers clean anatomy and sharp local structure (31.61 dB, SSIM 0.887), and it remains competitive in the limited-angle settings (iii–iv). However, it is much less robust to measurement noise. In the mild Poisson case (ii), its performance drops to 23.13 dB/0.342, with strong noise patterns in the lung region and a clear loss of vertebral structure in the cyan zoom. DPS and PGDM are more stable under noise, achieving 28.73 dB/0.802 and 28.28 dB/0.795 in setting (ii), respectively. However, their zoomed regions show residual smoothing and less distinct boundary detail, especially in the limited-angle cases. ReSample performs strongly in the sparse-view settings, reaching 32.32 dB/0.872 in setting (i) and 29.21 dB/0.780 in setting (ii), but degrades more substantially when the angular range is restricted. In setting (iv), it drops to 25.27 dB/0.768, and the zoomed regions show clear directional blurring. This suggests that hard projection is effective for sparse-view consistency, but is less sufficient when the null-space is structured by limited angular coverage.
The proposed method gives the strongest overall performance in the first four settings. It achieves 33.32 dB/0.897 in setting (i), 29.84 dB/0.846 in setting (ii), 33.19 dB/0.893 in setting (iii), and 29.17 dB/0.843 in setting (iv), giving the best PSNR and SSIM in each of these cases. These quantitative gains are consistent with the visual comparison: the proposed method better suppresses noise amplification, reduces directional artifacts, and preserves sharper anatomical boundaries in both zoomed regions. The advantage is particularly clear in the limited-angle setting (iii), where the proposed method outperforms the strongest competing posterior baseline, DDS, by 1.86 dB and produces sharper local structure in the vertebral zoom. This trend remains visible in the more severe limited-angle setting (iv). Although the margin is smaller, the proposed method still achieves the best PSNR and SSIM among the posterior methods, reaching 29.17 dB/0.843.
The most challenging setting (v), with only 10 noisy projections, shows a different trade-off. DPS obtains a slightly higher PSNR than the proposed method (27.42 dB versus 27.26 dB), but the proposed method achieves the highest SSIM among the posterior methods (0.786). Visually, DPS retains slightly more pixelwise contrast, but also shows residual streak artifacts, whereas the proposed method produces a smoother and more coherent reconstruction. This indicates that, in the most severely ill-posed noisy setting, PSNR and SSIM emphasise different aspects of reconstruction quality. In setting (v), the measurements are both extremely sparse and noisy. Under such conditions, hard or near-hard consistency, as used by ReSample and the proposed method, can preserve noise-related errors from the sinogram. This may limit PSNR, while the posterior mean still improves structural consistency, leading to high SSIM values.
Overall, the visual and quantitative results support the benefit of
geometry-aware sampling. The measurement-consistency step stabilises the range-space component of the reconstruction, while the pullback-metric-guided diffusion update improves the weakly constrained directions where the learned prior is most useful. This combination helps reduce streak artifacts, preserve anatomical boundaries, and produce stable posterior-mean reconstructions across sparse-view, noisy, and limited-angle settings.

4.3 Geometry-aware analysis
The previous sections evaluated reconstruction quality using PSNR and SSIM
(Section 4.1) and characterised empirical
sample variability through standard-deviation maps
(Section 4.2). While these results quantify
reconstruction fidelity and show where the generated reconstructions vary,
they do not fully explain how the acquisition geometry shapes the
reconstructions or their variability. To provide this geometric perspective,
we next perform a geometry-aware analysis using regularized range–null
component diagnostics.
First, we validate the regularized range–null component analysis on
representative reconstructions and confirm that the extracted null-like
component is effectively invisible to the measurement operator
(Section 4.3.1). Second, we apply the
decomposition to stochastic reconstructions and examine whether their sample
variability is concentrated in approximately measurement-invisible directions,
as predicted by the geometry of the method
(Section 4.3.2). Third, we isolate the role of
pullback-metric preconditioning by comparing the proposed update with a
Euclidean drift baseline, verifying that the geometry-aware update
preferentially moves along weakly constrained directions while maintaining
measurement consistency (Section 4.3.3). Finally, we
evaluate the effect of signal-dependent precision weighting derived from the
Poisson photon-counting noise model and demonstrate its benefit under
realistic CT noise (Section 4.3.4).
4.3.1 Regularized Range–Null Component Analysis
Before analysing variability across stochastic reconstructions, we examine
how the sample-mean reconstruction separates into measurement-sensitive and
weakly constrained components. This analysis is motivated by the exact
orthogonal direct-sum structure in
Proposition 1, but avoids explicitly
computing a pseudoinverse.
We define the regularized range-component operator
PR,γdec:=AΩ⊤​(AΩ​AΩ⊤+γdec​Im)−1​AΩ,γdec>0,P_{R,\gamma_{\mathrm{dec}}}\;:=\;A_{\Omega}^{\top}\left(A_{\Omega}A_{\Omega}^{\top}+\gamma_{\mathrm{dec}}I_{m}\right)^{-1}A_{\Omega},\qquad\gamma_{\mathrm{dec}}>0,
(58)
and its complementary null-like component operator
PN,γdec:=In−PR,γdec.P_{N,\gamma_{\mathrm{dec}}}\;:=\;I_{n}-P_{R,\gamma_{\mathrm{dec}}}.
(59)
Because the exact range–null decomposition is determined by AΩA_{\Omega}, we
use the unweighted Euclidean operator in this diagnostic. This isolates the
effect of the acquisition geometry from the noise weighting used during

(61)

4.3.4 Effect of Signal-Dependent Precision Weighting
We finally examine the effect of the signal-dependent precision weighting used in the proposed method. In the Poisson photon-counting model of CT, different sinogram entries have different noise levels: rays with lower photon counts are less reliable than rays with higher photon counts. Following the Gaussian approximation described in Section 3.6 and Appendix A.1, we model this using a signal-dependent covariance
Σ^−1=diag⁡(w1,…,wm),\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}\;=\;\operatorname{diag}(w_{1},\ldots,w_{m}),
(80)
where wi>0w_{i}>0 is a stabilized approximation of the estimated photon
count λ^i=I0​e−yi\widehat{\lambda}_{i}=I_{0}e^{-y_{i}} for the ii-th sinogram entry;
the precise construction is given in Appendix A.1.
This signal-dependent covariance enters the proposed method in three places. First, it appears in the weighted data-fidelity term
Φ(x)=12‖Σ^−1/2(AΩx−y)‖22.\Phi(x)\;=\;\frac{1}{2}\left\|\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1/2}(A_{\Omega}x-y)\right\|_{2}^{2}.
(81)
Second, it appears in the pullback metric
Gγ=AΩ⊤​Σ^−1​AΩ+γ​I.G_{\gamma}\;=\;A_{\Omega}^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}A_{\Omega}+\gamma I.
(82)
Third, the same metric defines the geometry-aware Langevin perturbation,
νG(k)∼𝒩⁡(0,2​αk​Gγ−1).\nu_{G}^{(k)}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(0,2\alpha_{k}G_{\gamma}^{-1}\right).
(83)

Both variants use the same pretrained diffusion prior, pullback-metric preconditioning, projection step, initialization, and hyperparameters. The only difference is whether the method uses the physically motivated signal-dependent covariance Σ^\widehat{\Sigma} or the unit covariance II. We evaluate the two variants under three 40-projection settings: no noise, Gaussian noise, and Poisson noise. The Poisson setting is the case where signal-dependent weighting is expected to matter most.

Figure 6 shows that signal-dependent precision has little effect when the measurement noise is absent or spatially uniform. In the noise-free setting, the two variants of our method are identical, giving 33.32 dB/0.897. The same is true under Gaussian noise, where both variants achieve 31.62 dB/0.867. This is expected: when all sinogram entries have the same noise level, unit precision is an appropriate model.
The situation changes under Poisson noise. FBP is strongly corrupted by noise, and DDS also degrades substantially to 23.08 dB/0.339. DPS is more stable, reaching 28.62 dB/0.800. The unit-precision version of our method performs worse than DPS, with 28.23 dB/0.691. This shows that using the wrong noise model can reduce the benefit of the geometry-aware update: if all sinogram bins are treated as equally reliable, noisy low-photon-count measurements can be over-weighted.
With signal-dependent precision, the proposed method improves to 29.84 dB/0.846. This is a gain of 1.61 dB and 0.155 SSIM over the unit-precision variant, and it also outperforms DPS by 1.22 dB and 0.046 SSIM. The improvement is visible in the cyan zoomed region around the vertebral body. The unit-precision reconstruction contains stronger noise-driven artifacts and a less clear cortical bone boundary, whereas the signal-dependent reconstruction gives a cleaner and sharper structure.
This behaviour is consistent with the Poisson noise model. Rays passing through high-attenuation tissue, such as bone, produce fewer detected photons and therefore have higher measurement variance. The signal-dependent precision down-weights these less reliable measurements in both the data-fidelity term and the pullback metric. As a result, the reconstruction is less dominated by noisy sinogram entries and relies more appropriately on the learned prior in uncertain regions.
These results show that signal-dependent precision is important for realistic Poisson CT noise. At the same time, it is complementary to the geometry-aware part of the method. In the noise-free and Gaussian-noise settings, where signal-dependent weighting provides no additional advantage, the proposed method already performs strongly. Under Poisson noise, the weighting becomes necessary to preserve this advantage by matching the data-consistency term to the physical noise model.
5 Conclusion and Future Work
We presented a geometry-aware posterior sampling method for sparse-view and limited-angle CT reconstruction. The method combines a diffusion prior with data consistency and a pullback-metric update. Instead of applying the diffusion update directly in image space, we use the measurement-induced metric GγG_{\gamma}
to adapt the update direction to the CT acquisition geometry. This reduces updates that would strongly change the measured sinogram, while allowing the learned prior to act in directions that are weakly constrained by the measurements.
The experiments show that this geometry-aware design improves reconstruction quality across sparse-view, noisy, and limited-angle CT settings. The proposed method reduces streak artifacts, suppresses noise amplification, and preserves anatomical boundaries more clearly than the posterior-sampling baselines considered. The pullback update also improves PSNR and SSIM compared with the Euclidean update, while keeping the measurement residual very small. The update-direction analysis further shows that the pullback metric reduces the measurement response of the deterministic update and redirects it toward approximately measurement-invisible directions.
The method also provides posterior uncertainty estimates. The posterior mean gives the final reconstruction, while the empirical sample standard-deviation map shows where the reconstruction is uncertain. In our experiments, Uncertainty is highest near anatomical boundaries, fine structures, and noise-sensitive regions, as well as structures weakly constrained by missing angular coverage in limited-angle CT. These regions often coincide with larger reconstruction errors, suggesting that the maps capture reconstruction ambiguity. By showing which structures are less reliably determined by the measured data, the maps improve the interpretability of learned reconstructions in ambiguous inverse problems.
Several directions remain for future work. First, although we test Poisson-corrupted measurements, the current posterior model uses a Gaussian approximation to the CT noise model. This approximation is reasonable when photon counts are not too low, but it may become inaccurate in very low-dose CT. A natural extension is to use the Poisson likelihood more directly. In that case, the data-fidelity term and the pullback metric would depend on the predicted photon counts at the current reconstruction. This could give a more accurate uncertainty model in strongly noise-dominated settings.
Second, the current method requires iterative linear solves to apply the inverse pullback metric during the reverse process. This makes the method more expensive than a Euclidean update. Future work should therefore develop faster matrix-free methods for applying Gγ−1G_{\gamma}^{-1}.
Possible directions include preconditioned conjugate gradients, warm-starting the solver across reverse iterations, adaptive stopping criteria, multigrid methods, low-rank approximations, and learned approximations of the inverse metric. Faster solvers would make the method easier to scale to larger images and three-dimensional CT volumes.
Finally, future work will consider latent-space versions of the method, to accelerate reconstruction speed, which would require a latent pullback metric.

Appendix A Theoretical Support
A.1 Gaussian Approximation of CT Measurements
This subsection motivates the approximate linear–Gaussian model
y=AΩ​x+ε,ε∼𝒩⁡(0,Σ),y=A_{\Omega}x+\varepsilon,\qquad\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma),
used in the main text. The derivation starts from an idealized Poisson
photon-counting model and explains the diagonal, signal-dependent covariance
of the post-log measurements. It also describes the fixed plug-in precision
used during reconstruction.
A.1.1 Poisson photon-counting model
Under a monoenergetic Beer–Lambert model, the expected photon count at
detector bin ii is [58]
λi=I0​e−(AΩ​x)i,\lambda_{i}\;=\;I_{0}e^{-(A_{\Omega}x)_{i}},
(85)
where I0I_{0} is the incident photon count, x∈ℝnx\in\mathbb{R}^{n} is the image of
linear-attenuation coefficients, and (AΩ​x)i(A_{\Omega}x)_{i} is the line integral
for ray ii. The detected count is modeled as
Yi∼Poisson⁡(λi),𝔼⁡[Yi]=λi,Var⁡(Yi)=λi.Y_{i}\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda_{i}),\qquad\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}]=\lambda_{i},\qquad\operatorname{Var}(Y_{i})=\lambda_{i}.
(86)
Thus, the variance of the raw count depends on its expected value.
This model is an idealization. Real CT measurements may additionally include
electronic noise, scatter, beam hardening, detector correlations, and effects
introduced by calibration and preprocessing [58, 59].
A.1.2 Gaussian approximation of Poisson counts
For a sufficiently large expected transmitted count, a Poisson random
variable is well approximated by a Gaussian random variable with the same
mean and variance.

Equivalently, for sufficiently large λ\lambda,

A.1.4 Post-log covariance and likelihood

conditionally independent, the post-log errors are approximately
independent. Stacking the measurements gives

Var⁡(εi)≈1λi=exp⁡((AΩ​x)i)I0.\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_{i})\;\approx\;\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}}\;=\;\frac{\exp((A_{\Omega}x)_{i})}{I_{0}}.

A.1.5 Fixed effective precision
The ideal post-log precision depends on the unknown transmitted count
λi=I0​exp⁡[−(AΩ​x)i]\lambda_{i}=I_{0}\exp[-(A_{\Omega}x)_{i}]. An ideal count-based plug-in estimate
would be
λ^i=I0​e−yi.\widehat{\lambda}_{i}\;=\;I_{0}e^{-y_{i}}.
(90)
In the implementation, we instead use a stabilized measurement-dependent
precision proxy. Zero detected counts are replaced by one count before
applying the logarithm. From the resulting post-log measurement yiy_{i}, we
first compute
wiraw=1yi+ϵ.w_{i}^{\mathrm{raw}}\;=\;\frac{1}{y_{i}+\epsilon}.
(91)
The weights are then normalized, scaled, and clipped:
wi=clip⁡(s​wiraw1m​∑j=1mwjraw+δ,wmin,wmax),w_{i}\;=\;\operatorname{clip}\left(s\,\frac{w_{i}^{\mathrm{raw}}}{\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{j}^{\mathrm{raw}}+\delta},w_{\min},w_{\max}\right),
(92)
where ϵ>0\epsilon>0 prevents division by small values, δ=10−8\delta=10^{-8}
stabilizes the mean normalization, s>0s>0 controls the overall precision
scale, and wminw_{\min} and wmaxw_{\max} limit the dynamic range.
We define the fixed effective precision and covariance by
Σ^−1:=diag⁡(w1,…,wm),Σ^:=diag⁡(w1−1,…,wm−1).\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}\;:=\;\operatorname{diag}(w_{1},\ldots,w_{m}),\qquad\widehat{\Sigma}\;:=\;\operatorname{diag}(w_{1}^{-1},\ldots,w_{m}^{-1}).
(93)
Once computed from the measured sinogram, these matrices are held fixed
throughout reconstruction. Unless stated otherwise, subsequent occurrences
of Σ\Sigma denote this fixed effective covariance
Σ^\widehat{\Sigma}, including the normalization and clipping above.
The resulting weights should be interpreted as a stabilized
measurement-dependent precision approximation rather than an unbiased
estimator of the ideal Poisson precision.
Validity regime.
The Gaussian approximation is most accurate when all relevant transmitted
counts are sufficiently large. At low counts, the normal approximation
deteriorates, the logarithmic bias becomes appreciable, and zero-count
handling becomes important. A pre-log Poisson model or a hybrid pre-/post-log
model can be preferable in that regime
[58, 55].

This subsection concerns the ideal continuous-time process with an exact,

finite-step practical algorithm, which uses a DDPM score approximation and
