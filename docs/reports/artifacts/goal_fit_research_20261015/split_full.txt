Abstract
Machine learning has achieved impressive performance in tomographic reconstruction, but supervised training requires paired measurements and ground-truth images that are often unavailable. This has motivated self-supervised approaches, which have primarily addressed denoising and, more recently, linear inverse problems. We address nonlinear inverse problems and introduce SPLIT (Self-supervised Partitioning for Learned Inversion in Nonlinear Tomography), a self-supervised machine-learning framework for reconstructing images from nonlinear, incomplete, and noisy projection data without any samples of ground-truth images. SPLIT enforces cross-partition consistency and measurement-domain fidelity while exploiting complementary information across multiple partitions. Our main theoretical result shows that, under mild conditions, the proposed self-supervised objective is equivalent to its supervised counterpart in expectation. We regularize training with an automatic stopping rule that halts optimization when a no-reference image-quality surrogate saturates. As a concrete application, we derive SPLIT variants for multispectral computed tomography. Experiments on sparse-view acquisitions demonstrate high reconstruction quality and robustness to noise, surpassing classical iterative reconstruction and recent self-supervised baselines.
Keywords.
Self-supervised learning, nonlinear inverse problems, tomographic reconstruction, multispectral CT, nonlinear imaging, material decomposition, Radon transform, Poisson noise, sparsity

1 Introduction
Accurate image reconstruction is a fundamental task in computed tomography (CT) and elsewhere, critical for medical diagnosis and industrial applications. In this paper, we study general tomographic reconstruction problems of the form of a nonlinear inverse problem
y=𝒜⁡(x)+η,y=\mathcal{A}(x)+\eta,
(1)
where 𝒜:X→Y\mathcal{A}\colon X\to Y is the (possibly) nonlinear forward operator between finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces, x∈Xx\in X the unknown image, and y∈Yy\in Y the observed measurement data. The term η∈Y\eta\in Y models potentially signal-dependent measurement noise; in practice, photon statistics are often Poisson and electronic noise is additive. While this work is motivated by and tested on multispectral CT (MSCT), the proposed reconstruction framework is general, with focus on non-linear tomography.
Classical linear tomography relies on inverting the linear Radon transform, which models internal structure as a scalar-valued discrete attenuation map μ∈ℝN\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{N} recovered from a noisy version of its sinogram ℛ​μ∈ℝP\mathcal{R}\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{P}, where ℛ\mathcal{R} denotes the discrete Radon transform. While effective in many settings, this linear model neglects crucial physical realities: the polychromatic nature of X-ray sources and the energy-dependent attenuation of materials. Real samples are better described by a family of attenuation maps μ⁡(e)∈ℝN\mu(e)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}, parameterized by photon energy e∈(0,∞)e\in(0,\infty). Using a single energy bin merges these distinct maps into a single projection, causing non-uniqueness and severe artifacts such as beam hardening. Although iterative and analytic correction methods exist [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6], they only partially mitigate these issues for a single X-ray source. Multispectral CT (MSCT) addresses these challenges by acquiring projection data across multiple energy bands, enabling reconstruction of energy-dependent attenuation maps. This yields a fundamentally nonlinear and more complex inverse problem [7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15].
The MSCT image reconstruction problem can be written in the form (1), where the unknown x=(x⁡[1],…,x⁡[M])∈X=ℝN×Mx=(x[1],\dots,x[M])\in X=\mathbb{R}^{N\times M} consists of a stack of material density images and the data y=(y⁡[1],…,y⁡[B])∈Y=ℝP×By=(y[1],\dots,y[B])\in Y=\mathbb{R}^{P\times B} are multispectral measurements across BB energy channels. The forward map 𝒜=Φ∘ℛ\mathcal{A}=\Phi\circ\mathcal{R} combines the Radon transform ℛ:ℝN×M→ℝP×M\mathcal{R}\colon\mathbb{R}^{N\times M}\to\mathbb{R}^{P\times M} with a pointwise nonlinearity Φ:ℝM→ℝB\Phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{B} that maps, per ray, MM line integrals to BB spectral measurements. Classical analytic and iterative methods, as well as supervised learning, have been applied to (1), but supervised approaches require paired (x,y)(x,y), which are difficult to obtain. Self-supervised methods [16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23] alleviate this by training only from measurements yy. In particular, relatives of our approach have shown success in denoising [16], full-data inverse problems [17], and linear settings with fixed, undersampled operators [18, 20]. However, to the best of our knowledge, no self-supervised approaches exist for nonlinear tomography.
In this paper, we propose a self-supervised framework for tomographic inverse problems that addresses nonlinear forward models, undersampling, and noise. We introduce SPLIT (Self-supervised Partitioning for Learned Inversion in Nonlinear Tomography), which allows multiple data splits. SPLIT partitions the data domain, targets reconstruction in image space, and employs a loss in the measurement domain. In its single-partition version, similar to Sparse2Inverse [18], SPLIT uses one partition along the angular dimension. To adequately account for noise and undersampling, we also introduce Double-SPLIT variants, which introduce a second partition along the detector (projection) dimension. We consider these methods within the SPLIT formulation for general nonlinear problems, potentially addressing multiple challenges present in the data. To the best of our knowledge, such multi-partition strategies have not been applied in self-supervised reconstruction before. We implement SPLIT for MSCT with a partition size of two in each dimension and demonstrate improvements over the self-supervised baseline. While our primary target is sparse-angle MSCT, these approaches apply to any linear or non-linear forward model that admits multiple data partitions.
2 SPLIT Framework
In this section we introduce the proposed SPLIT technique. We consider general inverse problems (1), where 𝒜:ℝN→ℝP\mathcal{A}\colon\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{P} is a deterministic possibly nonlinear forward map. We assume the unknown xx and the noise η\eta are random and follow unknown distributions pxp_{x} and pηp_{\eta}. The proposed SPLIT technique is inspired by Sparse2Inverse; however, it is applicable to nonlinear problems. Moreover, opposed to Sparse2Inverse we allow multiple partitions, which, to the best of our knowledge, has not been explored in the literature on self-supervised imaging.
2.1 Self-supervised imaging
Recently, self-supervised methods have been developed for linear inverse problems: Noise2Self for denoising, Noise2Inverse for full-data inverse problems, and Sparse2Inverse for limited-data inverse problems. Throughout this subsection we assume that x∈X=ℝNx\in X=\mathbb{R}^{N} and y∈Y=ℝPy\in Y=\mathbb{R}^{P} are random variables with joint distribution Px,yP_{x,y}. For any z∈ℝPz\in\mathbb{R}^{P} and any Ω⊆{1,…,P}\Omega\subseteq\{1,\dots,P\}, denote
zΩ=(zp)p∈Ω,zΩc=(zp)p∉Ω.z_{\Omega}=(z_{p})_{p\in\Omega},\qquad z_{\Omega}^{c}=(z_{p})_{p\notin\Omega}.
Self-supervised imaging methods are based on diagonal-free maps with respect to certain partitions of {1,…,P}\{1,\dots,P\} as outlined in the following.
•
Diagonal-free maps:
The crucial concept of self-supervised methods is diagonal-free nonlinear maps. Diagonal-free nonlinear maps, termed 𝒥\mathcal{J}-invariant in the Noise2Self framework [16] for self-supervised denoising, formalize the idea that each output coordinate must not depend on the corresponding input coordinate. Closely related structural constraints on operators appear in functional analysis and numerical analysis through off-diagonal or neighbor-only operators and sparsity patterns [24], as well as in dynamical systems through coupling structures and coordinate-wise dependence restrictions in discrete-time maps [25].
Definition 1 (Diagonal free nonlinear maps).
Let g:ℝP→ℝPg\colon\mathbb{R}^{P}\to\mathbb{R}^{P},
Λ⊆{1,…,P}\Lambda\subseteq\{1,\dots,P\} and 𝒫\mathcal{P} be a partition of {1,…,P}\{1,\dots,P\}. The function gg is called Λ\Lambda-self-invariant if
g​(z)Λ​ depends only on ​zΛcg(z)_{\Lambda}\text{ depends only on }z_{\Lambda}^{c}. It is called diagonal-free (with respect to 𝒫\mathcal{P}) if it is Ω\Omega-self-invariant for every Ω∈𝒫\Omega\in\mathcal{P}.
As a simple example consider P=3P=3 and a nonlinear map g:ℝ3→ℝ3g\colon\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3} defined by
g⁡(z1,z2,z3)=(sin⁡(z2+z3)z1+exp⁡(z3)cos⁡(z1−z2)).g(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})=\begin{pmatrix}\sin(z_{2}+z_{3})\\
z_{1}+\exp(z_{3})\\
\cos(z_{1}-z_{2})\end{pmatrix}.
Then, with the partition 𝒫={{1},{2},{3}}\mathcal{P}=\{\{1\},\{2\},\{3\}\}, gg is diagonal-free: each component gig_{i} depends only on the other two components with zjz_{j} with j∈{1,2,3}∖{i}j\in\{1,2,3\}\setminus\{i\} and not on ziz_{i} itself. ∀Ω∈𝒫:(g⁡(y))Ω≔(f⁡(ℳΩ​hΩ​(ℳΩc​y)+ℳΩc​y))Ω,\forall\Omega\in\mathcal{P}\colon\qquad\big(g(y)\big)_{\Omega}\coloneqq\big(f\big(\mathcal{M}_{\Omega}\,h_{\Omega}(\mathcal{M}_{\Omega}^{c}y)+\mathcal{M}_{\Omega}^{c}\,y\big)\big)_{\Omega}\,,
(2)
where ℳΩ,ℳΩc∈{0,1}P×P\mathcal{M}_{\Omega},\mathcal{M}_{\Omega}^{c}\in\{0,1\}^{P\times P} are diagonal masking matrices that zero out entries outside or inside Ω\Omega, respectively. Then, by construction, gg is diagonal-free with respect to 𝒫\mathcal{P}. In typical applications, ℳΩ​hΩ​(ℳΩc​y)\mathcal{M}_{\Omega}\,h_{\Omega}(\mathcal{M}_{\Omega}^{c}\,y) is either the zero function or a classical interpolation (e.g., local averaging) from the complement Ωc\Omega^{c} to the target region Ω\Omega, and ff is a neural network whose diagonal-free variant is given by gg.
•
Noise2Self: Consider first the denoising problem, which is (1) with 𝒜=id\mathcal{A}=\mathrm{id} and P=NP=N. The reconstruction function gg is then a denoiser, ideally minimizing the supervised risk 𝔼​‖g⁡(y)−x‖2\mathbb{E}\|g(y)-x\|^{2}. Instead, Noise2Self minimizes the self-supervised loss 𝔼​‖g⁡(y)−y‖2\mathbb{E}\|g(y)-y\|^{2} over diagonal-free functions. As shown in the seminal work [16], this is justified by the following result.
Proposition 1 (Noise2Self).
Let η\eta be pairwise independent with zero mean and let g:ℝN→ℝNg\colon\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N} be diagonal-free with respect to 𝒫\mathcal{P}. Then, 𝔼​‖g⁡(y)−y‖2=𝔼​‖g⁡(y)−x‖2+𝔼​‖η‖2\mathbb{E}\|g(y)-y\|^{2}\;=\;\mathbb{E}\|g(y)-x\|^{2}\;+\;\mathbb{E}\|\eta\|^{2}.
Based on Prop. 1, the risk 𝔼​‖g⁡(y)−x‖2\mathbb{E}\|g(y)-x\|^{2} is minimized by the same 𝒫\mathcal{P}-invariant function as the self-supervised risk 𝔼​‖g⁡(y)−y‖2\mathbb{E}\|g(y)-y\|^{2}. As demonstrated in [16], the performance of these estimators is very close to supervised ones.
•
Noise2Inverse:
Next, consider linear inverse problems written in the form (1) with a linear map 𝒜\mathcal{A}. Noise2Self can be applied either in the measurement domain for denoising yy followed by image formation, or in the reconstruction domain for denoising an initial reconstruction ℬ​y\mathcal{B}y. Both approaches suffer from a two-step nature and further drawbacks, such as correlation of the noise in the reconstruction domain XX and less informative prior in the measurement domain YY. To address these issues, [17] proposes Noise2Inverse, specifically where 𝒜\mathcal{A} is the full-view Radon transform. Here, a partition 𝒫\mathcal{P} is used in the projection domain where the noise is statistically independent, and a network is trained in the reconstruction domain. For any subset Ω∈𝒫\Omega\in\mathcal{P}, Noise2Inverse considers linear reconstructions ℬΩ,ℬΩc\mathcal{B}_{\Omega},\mathcal{B}_{\Omega}^{c} using data yΩ,yΩcy_{\Omega},y_{\Omega}^{c}. Then a network f:ℝN→ℝNf\colon\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N} is constructed by minimizing
𝔼​‖f⁡(ℬΩc​yΩc)−ℬΩ​yΩ‖2\mathbb{E}\|f(\mathcal{B}_{\Omega}^{c}y_{\Omega}^{c})-\mathcal{B}_{\Omega}y_{\Omega}\|^{2}.
Noise2Inverse is supported by the following result derived in [17].
Proposition 2 (Noise2Inverse).
Suppose η\eta has zero mean and is pairwise independent. Then, for any f:ℝN→ℝNf\colon\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}, we have
𝔼​‖f⁡(ℬΩc​yΩc)−ℬΩ​yΩ‖2=𝔼​‖f⁡(ℬΩc​yΩc)−ℬΩ​𝒜Ω​x‖2+𝔼​‖ℬΩ​η‖2\mathbb{E}\|f(\mathcal{B}_{\Omega}^{c}y_{\Omega}^{c})-\mathcal{B}_{\Omega}y_{\Omega}\|^{2}=\mathbb{E}\|f(\mathcal{B}_{\Omega}^{c}y_{\Omega}^{c})-\mathcal{B}_{\Omega}\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}x\|^{2}+\mathbb{E}\|\mathcal{B}_{\Omega}\eta\|^{2}.
Based on Prop. 2, the supervised risk
𝔼​‖f⁡(ℬΩc​yΩc)−ℬΩ​𝒜Ω​x‖2\mathbb{E}\|f(\mathcal{B}_{\Omega}^{c}y_{\Omega}^{c})-\mathcal{B}_{\Omega}\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}x\|^{2}
and the self-supervised risk
𝔼​‖f⁡(ℬΩc​yΩc)−ℬΩ​yΩ‖2\mathbb{E}\|f(\mathcal{B}_{\Omega}^{c}y_{\Omega}^{c})-\mathcal{B}_{\Omega}y_{\Omega}\|^{2}
share the same minimizer. After computing the minimizer within a chosen function class, inference is done by
ℬ⁡(y)≔|𝒫|−1​∑Ω∈𝒫f⁡(ℬΩc​yΩc)\mathcal{B}(y)\coloneq|\mathcal{P}|^{-1}\sum_{\Omega\in\mathcal{P}}f(\mathcal{B}_{\Omega}^{c}y_{\Omega}^{c}).
•
Sparse2Inverse:
Noise2Inverse aims to remove measurement noise but is not designed to remove artifacts resulting from undersampling in the case of sparse data, when 𝒜\mathcal{A} has a large null space. The reason is that the targets in this framework are defined using linear reconstruction methods and thus still fix the null-space component shared by any of the maps ℬΩ​𝒜Ω\mathcal{B}_{\Omega}\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}. To overcome this issue, in [18] we presented a method that uses a network in reconstruction domain but with a loss in the measurement domain (also see [20]).
More specifically, Sparse2Inverse minimizes
𝔼​‖𝒜Ω​f​(ℬΩc​yΩc)−yΩ‖2\mathbb{E}\|\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}f(\mathcal{B}_{\Omega}^{c}y_{\Omega}^{c})-y_{\Omega}\|^{2}
based on the following result.
Proposition 3 (Sparse2Inverse).
Suppose η\eta is pairwise independent with zero mean.
Then for any f:ℝN→ℝNf\colon\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}, we have
𝔼​‖𝒜Ω​f​(ℬΩc​yΩc)−yΩ‖2=𝔼​‖𝒜Ω​f​(ℬΩc​yΩc)−𝒜Ω​x‖2+𝔼​‖η‖2\mathbb{E}\|\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}f(\mathcal{B}_{\Omega}^{c}y_{\Omega}^{c})-y_{\Omega}\|^{2}=\mathbb{E}\|\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}f(\mathcal{B}_{\Omega}^{c}y_{\Omega}^{c})-\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}x\|^{2}+\mathbb{E}\|\eta\|^{2}.
Based on Prop. 3,
𝔼​‖𝒜Ω​f​(ℬΩc​yΩc)−𝒜Ω​x‖2\mathbb{E}\|\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}f(\mathcal{B}_{\Omega}^{c}y_{\Omega}^{c})-\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}x\|^{2}
and
𝔼​‖𝒜Ω​f​(ℬΩc​yΩc)−yΩ‖2\mathbb{E}\|\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}f(\mathcal{B}_{\Omega}^{c}y_{\Omega}^{c})-y_{\Omega}\|^{2}
share the same minimizer. After computing the minimizer within a chosen function class, inference can be done by
ℬ⁡(y)≔|𝒫|−1​∑Ω∈𝒫f⁡(ℬΩc​yΩc)\mathcal{B}(y)\coloneq|\mathcal{P}|^{-1}\sum_{\Omega\in\mathcal{P}}f\bigl(\mathcal{B}_{\Omega}^{c}y_{\Omega}^{c}\bigr).
The crucial difference compared to the loss in Noise2Inverse is the freedom of the network to operate in the null space of 𝒜\mathcal{A}. This is a prerequisite for the network to effectively remove undersampling artifacts inherent in ℬΩ​𝒜Ω\mathcal{B}_{\Omega}\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}. In numerical reconstruction, such a behavior in indeed observed [18, 19].
2.2 Proposed SPLIT
The SPLIT technique consists of several elements that we describe below. It is applicable to any linear or nonlinear inverse problem of the form (1). At its core, it uses multiple partitions in the measurement domain with the intention of addressing different challenges in (1), for example, missing data and denoising. We first derive it in a general framework for nonlinear problems with an arbitrary number of partitions and then specialize it to Double-SPLIT for MSCT.
•
Multi-partitioning:
In contrast to existing self-supervised approaches that use a single partition, we include multiple partitions of {1,…,P}\{1,\dots,P\} to potentially account for different tasks. The partitions are denoted by 𝒫i\mathcal{P}_{i} for i=1,…,Ki=1,\dots,K with elements Ωi,j⊆{1,…,P}\Omega_{i,j}\subseteq\{1,\dots,P\} for j=1,…,|𝒫i|j=1,\dots,|\mathcal{P}_{i}|. In the spirit of Noise2Inverse, one would use these for denoising with the norm in the reconstruction space ℝN\mathbb{R}^{N}, thereby fixing the null space component of the solution selection. For a single partition (K=1K=1), we refer to (10), (11) as single-SPLIT, which is the straightforward extension of Sparse2Inverse to nonlinear problems. The use of multiple partitions naturally allows different splits for different tasks, such as denoising and missing-data recovery; the Double-SPLIT employs two complementary partitions, such as angular and detector domains in MSCT.
2.3 Theoretical analysis
Similar to single-split methods for linear inverse problems, we show that the self-supervised data-space loss coincides with its supervised counterpart.
Theorem 1 (Multi-SPLIT).
Assume the measurement model (1) with a nonlinear forward map 𝒜:ℝN→ℝP\mathcal{A}\colon\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{P}, where the noise η\eta has pixel-wise independent mean-zero entries. Let (𝒫i)i=1K(\mathcal{P}_{i})_{i=1}^{K} be multiple data partitions and let the restricted data yi,j,yi,jcy_{i,j},y_{i,j}^{c} and partial forward and reconstruction maps 𝒜i,j,𝒜i,jc,ℬi,j,ℬi,jc\mathcal{A}_{i,j},\mathcal{A}_{i,j}^{c},\mathcal{B}_{i,j},\mathcal{B}_{i,j}^{c} be defined by (3)–(8). Proof.

𝔼​‖𝒜i,j​(f⁡(ℬi,jc​yi,jc))−yi,j‖2\mathbb{E}\|\mathcal{A}_{i,j}(f(\mathcal{B}_{i,j}^{c}y_{i,j}^{c}))-y_{i,j}\|^{2}.
Using y=𝒜⁡(x)+ηy=\mathcal{A}(x)+\eta yields

=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\bigl\|\mathcal{A}_{i,j}f(\mathcal{B}_{i,j}^{c}y_{i,j}^{c})-\mathcal{A}_{i,j}x\bigr\|^{2}\Bigr]+2\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\langle\mathcal{A}_{i,j}f(\mathcal{B}_{i,j}^{c}y_{i,j}^{c})-\mathcal{A}_{i,j}x,\eta_{i,j}\rangle\Bigr]+\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\eta_{i,j}\|^{2}\bigr].
(13)
Now 𝒜i,j​x\mathcal{A}_{i,j}x is independent of ηi,j\eta_{i,j} and ηi,j\eta_{i,j} has zero mean, thus 𝔼⁡[⟨𝒜i,j​x,ηi,j⟩]=0\mathbb{E}\big[\langle\mathcal{A}_{i,j}x,\eta_{i,j}\rangle\big]=0. Combining these with (13) and summing over all i,ji,j gives (12).
∎
We do not believe that a similar theorem can be derived for the Noise2Inverse-type loss (9); we conjecture this is due to the necessarily nonlinear nature of the reconstruction maps ℬi,j\mathcal{B}_{i,j}. Noise2Inverse for linear problems does not suffer from this limitation as long as linear reconstruction maps are used.
Theorem 1 may be interpreted as follows: For any i∈{1,…,N}i\in\{1,\dots,N\}, the map gi​(y)≔((𝒜i,j∘f∘ℬi,jc)​(yi,jc))j=1|𝒫i|g_{i}(y)\coloneqq((\mathcal{A}_{i,j}\circ f\circ\mathcal{B}_{i,j}^{c})(y_{i,j}^{c}))_{j=1}^{|\mathcal{P}_{i}|} is diagonal-free with respect to the partition 𝒫i\mathcal{P}_{i} in measurement space, enabling denoising there in the spirit of Noise2Self. The crucial ingredient for image reconstruction, however, is that the maps gig_{i} contain learned factors f∘ℬi,jcf\circ\mathcal{B}_{i,j}^{c} that map from measurement space to image space. The maps f∘ℬi,jcf\circ\mathcal{B}_{i,j}^{c} are then used to define a learned self-supervised reconstruction map via (11).
3 Application to MSCT
In this section we present a concrete realization of SPLIT for MSCT using one and two partitions, referred to as Single-SPLIT and Double-SPLIT, respectively. We present numerical details, experimental results, and comparisons with an iterative one-step algorithm and a self-supervised baseline.
3.1 MSCT forward and inverse model
We start with a brief derivation of the MSCT forward model, resulting in a nonlinear inverse problem of the form (1). This includes modeling spectral measurements of the energy-dependent attenuation distribution and combining them with a material representation along the energy dimension. Additionally, we present an efficient one-step reconstruction algorithm for MSCT, used in the implementation of SPLIT.
•
Spectral Radon transform:
Classical CT seeks to recover a scalar-valued discrete attenuation map μ∈ℝN\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{N} for the patient or sample from observations of its (linear) Radon transform ℛ​μ∈ℝP\mathcal{R}\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{P}, obtained by comparing incoming and outgoing X-ray intensity. However, the energy-dependent absorption characteristics of real materials and the broadband nature of X-ray sources necessitate a family of attenuation maps μ⁡(e,⋅)∈ℝN\mu(e,\cdot)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}, parameterized by photon energy e>0e>0, for accurate modeling. Moreover, taking into account the source spectrum of emitted X-rays and the detector’s spectral response leads to measurements of the form
y⁡(ℓ)≔∫0∞s⁡(e)​exp⁡(−(ℛ​μ​(e,ℓ)))​𝑑e,y(\ell)\coloneq\int_{0}^{\infty}s(e)\,\exp\!\bigl(-\,(\mathcal{R}\mu(e,\ell))\bigr)\,de\,,
(14)
where s⁡(e)s(e) is the effective spectrum (the product of the source spectrum and the detector spectral response), ℛ​μ​(e,ℓ)\mathcal{R}\mu(e,\ell) is the Radon transform of μ⁡(e,⋅)\mu(e,\cdot) in the spatial variable, and ℓ\ell is a ray of integration. Due to spectral mixing across energies, recovering (μ⁡(e,⋅))e>0(\mu(e,\cdot))_{e>0} from a single effective spectrum is severely ill-posed, leading to non-uniqueness and beam-hardening artifacts.
Multispectral CT (MSCT) mitigates these issues by acquiring projection data across multiple energy bands using distinct effective spectra (sb​(e))b=1B(s_{b}(e))_{b=1}^{B}. Assuming a low-dimensional model along the energy axis (e.g., a material basis; see (15) below), these measurements enable accurate reconstruction of energy-dependent attenuation.

Assuming a finite number of rays and a finite number of effective spectra, and discretizing the integral over the spectral variable, yields the MSCT measurements

Despite its simplicity, CP-fast has been demonstrated in [26] to converge rapidly with numerically cheap iteration steps, outperforming tested one-step algorithms compared in [11].
•
Learned MSCT reconstruction:
Let us also comment on existing work on learning for MSCT. Early works focused on supervised methods, including [27, 28, 29, 30]. More closely related to SPLIT are recently proposed self-supervised approaches for MSCT such as [31, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36]. However, all these methods are based on two-step reconstruction pipelines that separate image formation and material decomposition. In contrast, we target joint image formation and material decomposition in a single step, by combining one-step iterative image reconstruction with self-supervised learning. To the best of our knowledge, no self-supervised method in such a joint setting has been proposed.

Earlystopping:
The loss (25) is data-driven and exploits the available samples. Our proposed approach involves comparing Φθ​(ℬ⁡(yi,2))\Phi_{\theta}(\mathcal{B}(y_{i,2})) with Φθ​(ℬ⁡(yi,1))\Phi_{\theta}(\mathcal{B}(y_{i,1})) in terms of the Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR).

By monitoring these cross-domain output differences through the PSNR\psnr metric, we can detect the onset of overfitting.

We next specify details for the results presented, concerning MSCT specifications, the network training and the used data sets.
•
MSCT specifications:
The numerical settings for the MSCT forward map are taken as in [26], which uses the implementation of [11]. We consider M=3M=3 base materials (water, iodine, gadolinium) and B=5B=5 energy bins. 4 Conclusion
Learned image reconstruction has become state of the art across many imaging modalities. Of particular interest are self-supervised methods that require only measurement samples y=𝒜⁡(x)+ηy=\mathcal{A}(x)+\eta without access to paired data (x,y)(x,y) or even ground-truth images xx. While such approaches have primarily focused on linear problems, this work addresses the nonlinear, incomplete, and noisy regime relevant to nonlinear inverse problems. We introduced and realized SPLIT (Self-supervised Partitioning for Learned Inversion in Nonlinear Tomography) for one-step MSCT reconstruction and material decomposition. Our framework leverages measurement-domain losses, cross-partition consistency, and complementary information across spectral channels, together with an automatic stopping criterion that serves as regularization. SPLIT consistently improves reconstruction quality and robustness compared with iterative reconstruction baseline and a self-supervised baseline using a reconstruction-domain loss.
Future work will include evaluation on real MSCT datasets with realistic calibration and model mismatch, systematic studies of noise (e.g., Poisson and electronic noise) and sparsity regimes, and theoretical analysis of identifiability, stability, and convergence under nonlinear forward models. We also plan to integrate recent advances in physics-informed priors, quantify uncertainty, and extend the framework to other nonlinear and ill-posed inverse problems such as quantitative photoacoustic tomography and inverse diffusion (diffuse optical tomography).
